The Risk-Adjusted Backlog: How To Prioritise When Failure Costs Real Money

The standard PM prioritisation frameworks — RICE, ICE, WSJF, MoSCoW, Kano — all share a hidden assumption: shipping the wrong thing costs roughly nothing. You burn engineering time. You miss the quarter. You learn something. You move on.

The assumption is roughly true in SaaS. In payments it is structurally wrong. Shipping the wrong thing can cost the platform a scheme certification, an audit finding, a regulator sanction, a merchant lawsuit, a chargeback wave that breaches the band ceiling, a sev-1 incident that pulls executives away from the next quarter, or the licence itself.

The risk-adjusted backlog is the prioritisation discipline that takes the asymmetric downside seriously. It does not replace RICE; it sits on top of it. The senior payments PM who has been through one audit cycle has internalised it.

This is the operating definition: the five risk axes, the formula that does not pretend to be RICE, the four moves that earn the most lift, and the operating cadence that makes it stick.

What "risk-adjusted" actually means

A standard prioritisation formula gives every backlog item a score on upside only: how much value will this produce if it ships well. RICE is the cleanest example: (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort. Higher = better. The formula is symmetric about the value axis; downside enters only via the Confidence factor, which is a probability of delivery, not a probability of harm.

Risk-adjusted prioritisation adds the downside axis explicitly. Every backlog item gets two scores:

  • Value score. What this delivers if it ships well. RICE / WSJF / whichever value-side formula the team uses.
  • Downside score. What this costs if it ships badly. The cost when delivery still happens but the outcome is harm — to the licence, to merchants, to the audit posture, to the team's capacity.

The ranking is not "high value first" or "low risk first". It is "highest value-net-of-downside first", where the downside is real money in the same units as the upside.

The five downside axes in payments

A working set of axes. Each scored 1–5; the highest axis dominates.

1. Licence exposure. What is the worst plausible regulator action if this ships badly? 5 = central-bank suspension, 4 = formal warning + capital implications, 3 = audit finding requiring remediation, 2 = expected supervisory comment, 1 = no licence touch.

2. Scheme posture. What is the worst plausible scheme response? 5 = certification loss, 4 = mandatory remediation programme, 3 = scheme review under chargeback monitoring, 2 = scheme-specified observation, 1 = no scheme touch.

3. Customer / merchant impact. What is the worst plausible customer outcome? 5 = stranded funds, 4 = portfolio-wide outage, 3 = merchant-tier service degradation, 2 = isolated merchant disruption, 1 = invisible to customers.

4. Financial loss. What is the worst plausible direct financial cost? 5 = capital-impacting (>1% of equity), 4 = material P&L impact, 3 = noticeable expense line, 2 = absorbed in run-rate, 1 = negligible.

5. Reputational / contractual risk. Worst plausible reputational consequence? 5 = published regulator action / press coverage, 4 = merchant churn at scale, 3 = senior contractual escalation, 2 = isolated relationship damage, 1 = invisible.

The downside score for an item is the maximum across the five axes, not the average. A single 5 on any axis dominates; that is the asymmetric-downside reality of payments work.

The formula that does not pretend to be RICE

The standard RICE formula does not extend cleanly to downside. The risk-adjusted version is closer to expected-value:

Adjusted score = Value × Probability(good outcome) - Downside × Probability(bad outcome)

In practice, teams do not need a continuous probability. The senior PM uses a banded version:

Downside score Probability(bad outcome) Multiplier on the downside
1–2 ~5% Add as footnote, do not deduct
3 ~10–15% Deduct 10–15% × downside cost
4 ~20–30% Deduct 20–30% × downside cost
5 ~30–50% Deduct 30–50% × downside cost

Items with a downside-5 axis are not eliminated automatically. They are flagged for special treatment: more architectural review, more rollout staging, more pre-launch testing, and an explicit go/no-go gate that the CPO (and sometimes the CRO or General Counsel) signs.

The point of the formula is not the arithmetic. It is the posture. Items with high downside cannot be ranked next to items with low downside without acknowledging the asymmetry. The team that does not make the asymmetry visible defaults to "RICE rules" and ships the wrong things first.

Four moves that earn the most lift

Move 1 — Make the downside score visible in every prioritisation review

The simplest move. The backlog spreadsheet (or Linear / Jira / Productboard, whichever) has a "downside" column visible alongside RICE. Every item is scored in both. The ranking shows both scores. The conversation in the quarterly review explicitly tests trade-offs that ignoring downside would have hidden.

The behavioural effect is more important than the numerical effect. The team stops talking about "highest RICE" and starts talking about "highest value-net-of-downside".

Move 2 — Build an explicit "regulator / scheme deadline" lane

Items driven by a regulator or scheme deadline get a separate lane on the board. The lane is not graded on RICE; it is graded on delivery against the external date. Mixing deadline-bound items with discretionary items on the same ranked list produces two failure modes: either the deadlines crowd out everything (organisational lockup) or RICE-ranked items push the deadlines off the slate (regulatory exposure).

The deadline lane has its own capacity (often 30–50% of total engineering capacity in regulated quarters), its own ownership, its own status reporting. The RICE-ranked lane covers the discretionary budget.

Move 3 — Pre-mortem every downside-4-or-5 item

For every item with a downside score of 4 or 5, before the engineering work starts:

  • The PM and the lead engineer write a one-page pre-mortem: what could go wrong, what would the impact be, what is the rollback path.
  • The risk / compliance lead reviews and signs.
  • The CPO (and sometimes CRO / GC) explicitly approves the launch criteria.

The pre-mortem is the cheap protection: 2–4 hours of structured pessimism that prevents 50+ hours of incident response. It also produces the rollback documentation that the engineering team will need if the launch goes wrong.

Move 4 — Track the "did not ship because of downside" log

The discipline is incomplete until it is visible. The senior PM keeps a log of items that did not ship this quarter (or did not ship at all) because the downside math said no. The log is reviewed monthly by the leadership team.

Two functions:

  • It makes the discipline auditable. "We did not ship X because" is a stronger conversation than "we deprioritised it".
  • It builds the team's risk intuition over time. Junior PMs see why senior PMs made the calls they made; the rubric internalises faster.

What this is not

The risk-adjusted backlog is not an excuse to avoid risk. Payments product work is, by its nature, high-stakes work. The discipline is to price the risk, not to avoid it.

Three patterns to watch for, where teams use risk-adjusted framing as a hiding place:

1. Inflating downside on items the PM doesn't want to ship. The downside score becomes a veto tool. The check: are downside scores stable across reviews, or do they move when the PM wants a different outcome? If they move, the scoring is being gamed.

2. Using downside to slow regulator deadlines. "We can't ship this in 3 months, the downside on rushed work is too high." Sometimes true; often a way to push the deadline. The regulator wins the conversation; downside framing does not change the calendar.

3. Refusing to ship any downside-5 item. Some downside-5 items have to ship — they are mandatory. The discipline is to ship them with extra protection, not to refuse.

The senior PM uses the framework to make better calls, not to delay them.

The operating cadence

A working rhythm for keeping the framework alive:

  • Quarterly planning. Every backlog item is scored on value and downside. The slate is built with explicit risk-budget allocation between deadline-bound, RICE-ranked, and downside-flagged work.
  • Monthly review. Re-score items where the world has changed (regulator publishes, scheme updates rules, a major merchant escalates). Adjust the lane capacities if the deadline pressure has shifted.
  • Pre-launch gate. Every item with downside 4 or 5 has its pre-mortem reviewed and signed. No exception.
  • Post-launch post-mortem. For any item that produced an unexpected incident: was the downside score correct? Update the scoring rubric for future items.
  • Quarterly retrospective. Review the "did not ship because of downside" log. Were the calls right? What got better; what got worse.

The rituals matter. Frameworks that exist only on paper degrade in 60 days. Frameworks that are part of the team's regular cadence outlast the PM who introduced them.

The senior-PM tell

The interview question that distinguishes senior payments PMs on this: "your RICE-top item has a downside-5 axis on regulator exposure. The board is pushing to ship it. What do you do?"

The junior answer accepts the board's call. The senior answer does not refuse, but pushes back through the discipline: explicit pre-mortem; explicit rollout staging; explicit gate criteria from the CRO and the CPO before launch; explicit kill criteria during rollout. The item ships, but it ships with the downside priced. If the rollout shows the downside materialising, the senior PM kills it without apology, because the kill criteria were agreed upfront.

The conversation is not "should we ship this fast" vs "should we delay this". It is "what does shipping this responsibly look like, and what are the costs we accept if responsibility means a 30% chance of a 4-week delay". The senior PM owns the framing; the board owns the call.

FAQ

How is this different from WSJF? WSJF (Weighted Shortest Job First) handles cost-of-delay well, which maps to regulator deadlines. WSJF does not natively handle the asymmetric downside of payments work (failure modes that cost the licence). A WSJF + downside-axis hybrid is roughly what we are describing.

How is this different from Kano-style "must-haves"? Kano sorts features by user preference. The downside axes here are not about user preference; they are about systemic risk. A licence-protecting feature is not a Kano must-have because users may not even see it.

Does this make the backlog slower? Marginally. The extra scoring takes ~10 minutes per item; the pre-mortem on downside-4-or-5 items takes 2–4 hours. The throughput is comparable; the direction of throughput is different. Items that would have produced incidents are now caught at planning time.

What if the team disagrees on downside scores? Productive disagreement. The senior PM facilitates a calibration conversation; the goal is not consensus but visibility. Disagreement on downside often reveals that the team has different mental models of what the platform can absorb.

Does this map to non-regulated products? Partially. Any product where a failure produces step-function harm (financial services, healthcare, infrastructure, safety) benefits from the asymmetric framing. Products where failure is "we learn and iterate" do not need it.

Should engineering also score downside? Yes — engineering's view often catches risk axes that product misses (system-stability risk, dependency risk, technical-debt accumulation). The senior PM facilitates the joint scoring; the score is one number, but two reviewers calibrate it.
