Payments PRD Template: The Nine Sections Every Senior PM Writes

The SaaS PRD survived a decade of changing tools because its structure was almost universal: problem, goal, users, stories, scope, success metric, open questions. Eight headings, one page in good cases, three pages in long ones. The shape worked because the failure modes of a SaaS feature are mostly UX failures and the maths is mostly conversion.

A payments PRD shaped like that ships incidents.

The reason is not that payments PMs need to be more rigorous than SaaS PMs. It is that the failure modes of a payments feature live in places a SaaS PRD has no section for. Money movement timing. Scheme rules. Reconciliation symmetry. Audit posture. The PRD has to force the writer to think about each of those before code starts, because they are not catchable in QA.

This is the template that holds, after writing or reviewing more than a hundred payments PRDs across acquirer, processor, wallet, BNPL, cross-border, and KYB products. Nine sections. The shape is not negotiable. The depth of each section flexes with the size of the change.

Section 1 — Problem and context

Half a page. Two paragraphs.

The first paragraph names the problem in operator language, not feature language. "We want to add Apple Pay" is feature language; not a problem. "Mobile auth rate on iOS Safari is 6 points below desktop, and merchant ops attribute most of the lost authorisations to manual card-entry abandonment" is the problem. The PRD lives or dies in how this paragraph is written, because every later decision derives from it.

The second paragraph is the strategic context. Why now, not last quarter, not next. What changed externally (regulator deadline, scheme mandate, large merchant pressure, competitor move) or internally (capacity, prior work landed, telemetry signal). If the answer to "why now" is "we have time", deprioritise the PRD; it is not yet a problem worth shipping.

Prompt that forces clarity: "If I do not ship this in the next two quarters, what specifically gets worse?"

Section 2 — Goal and non-goals

One short paragraph plus two bullet lists.

The goal is a single sentence in product-outcome language, not feature language. "Add a 3DS2 step-up exemption flow" is feature; not a goal. "Lift iOS Safari auth rate to within 2 points of desktop without increasing the fraud rate" is a goal. The goal has a numeric target and a guard-rail.

Non-goals are equally important. The reason most payments PRDs spawn rework is that the engineering team interpreted scope wider than the PM intended. List the things this PRD is explicitly not solving — even when the team might want to. Non-goals are the future PRDs in the queue.

Prompt that forces clarity: "What is the simplest possible thing that would close this problem, and what am I explicitly choosing not to do beyond that?"

Section 3 — Money movement and timing

This is the section that does not exist in SaaS PRDs and where most payments PRDs fail.

For every flow the feature touches, the PRD walks through the money path:

  • Who pays whom. Cardholder → issuer → scheme → acquirer → merchant settlement bank → merchant. Name each leg even if it is obvious.
  • When the money moves. Authorisation hold (T+0), capture (T+0 or T+n), settlement (T+1 / T+2 / same-day), refund timing, chargeback timing.
  • What the failure recovery looks like. If the issuer auth succeeds but the capture fails, what happens? If the scheme settles but the merchant bank rejects, what happens? Name the recovery path for each.
  • Who carries the float. During the gap between authorisation and settlement, whose balance sheet is the money on?
  • What is reversible and what is not. Authorisations can be voided; captures cannot be voided after settlement; settled funds reverse only through dispute or refund. The PRD says which states the feature touches.

If this section is empty in your PRD, the feature has not been designed yet, regardless of how complete the rest of the document looks.

Prompt that forces clarity: "Draw the money path on a whiteboard with timing labels. If you cannot draw it from memory, you do not understand the feature yet."

Section 4 — Regulator and scheme posture

Every payments feature lives inside a regulatory and scheme envelope. The PRD names which licences and which scheme rulebooks the feature touches, and what each requires.

For regulator posture:

  • Which licence(s) cover this activity in which markets
  • Which articles or directives apply (PSD2 SCA, AMLD6, GDPR, market-specific central-bank circulars)
  • What customer disclosures are required
  • What records must be kept and for how long
  • What audit evidence must be produced and where it lives

For scheme posture:

  • Which scheme rules apply (Visa Core Rules, Mastercard Standards, scheme-equivalents for local rails)
  • Whether certification is required and what the certification path is
  • Whether the feature changes the BIN-routing logic, interchange category, or merchant category code
  • Whether the feature triggers a scheme attestation event

This section is the artefact that the legal and compliance review uses. If it is missing or thin, the review takes three rounds instead of one. If it is complete, the review is often a one-pass approval.

Prompt that forces clarity: "If a regulator or scheme auditor walked in tomorrow and asked 'show me your design evidence for this control', could you hand them this section as-is?"

Section 5 — Reconciliation and ledger impact

For every state transition the feature introduces, the PRD names the ledger entries and the reconciliation pairings.

  • Internal ledger entries. What account is debited, what is credited, with what reference, at what timestamp.
  • External statement pairings. Which scheme settlement file row, which bank statement entry, which correspondent message, will this transaction land in. The PRD names the field the matching engine will key on (UETR, ARN, scheme transaction ID, reference).
  • Reconciliation breaks. Which states could leave a ledger entry without a paired external row and how the ops team detects and resolves the break.
  • End-of-day posture. What the daily reconciliation report shows for this flow. Whether a break in this flow blocks settlement or is logged for next-day review.

Most payments incidents are ledger incidents discovered hours or days after the bad transaction. The PRD that names the reconciliation pairings before code prevents the incident; the PRD that does not, ships it.

Prompt that forces clarity: "If a regional finance team called at 9pm asking 'why is the ledger ten thousand dirhams off today,' can the on-call engineer trace the answer through the artefacts this PRD names?"

Section 6 — Failure modes and edge cases

Every payments PRD has a section listing what can go wrong and what the product does when it does.

Group failures into four categories:

Upstream failures. Scheme timeouts, issuer declines, gateway errors, network partitions. For each, the PRD specifies the retry policy, the merchant-facing error message, and the consumer-facing message.

Downstream failures. Settlement bank rejection, sponsor bank delays, correspondent issues, payout file failure. For each, the PRD specifies what state the transaction is left in and how the merchant is notified.

Adversarial cases. Fraud-triggered declines, scheme risk holds, regulator-mandated freezes, AML alerts. For each, the PRD specifies the customer experience, the case-management workflow, and the SLA for resolution.

Race conditions. Concurrent capture and refund, dispute opened while still settling, void after partial capture, refund before settlement. For each, the PRD specifies the precedence and the resulting ledger state.

If the team cannot fill this section, the team has not yet thought through what they are building. Edge cases are the substance of payments product work; the happy path is the easy half.

Prompt that forces clarity: "What is the worst outcome a customer can experience because of this feature, and what specifically prevents it?"

Section 7 — Telemetry, KPIs, and acceptance criteria

The metric the goal is written in is in here. So is every metric the team will watch on the way to that goal, and the acceptance criteria that mark the work as "done".

Three layers:

Primary KPIs. The numeric targets the PRD commits to. Authorisation rate at X%, settlement timing at Y, dispute cycle time at Z. Each KPI has a baseline reading, a target, and a measurement window.

Guard-rail KPIs. The metrics that must not move adversely. Fraud rate, refund-to-payment ratio, support contact rate, reconciliation break count. Each guard-rail has a threshold beyond which the rollout is paused.

Telemetry events. The instrumentation the engineering team builds to observe the feature in production. Event names, properties, downstream queries. The PRD names these so the engineering team does not invent them later from memory.

Acceptance criteria are written in past tense, observable, and checkable by the PO (per the CSPO discipline):

  • "The 3DS2 step-up flow has been tested with the four most common issuer combinations and has produced a successful auth in each."
  • "The new dispute portal has surfaced at least 95% of in-flight cases with correct status to ten reference merchants."
  • "The settlement reconciliation engine has zero breaks on the first three days of the new flow."

Prompt that forces clarity: "Could a junior PM, given only this section, decide whether the feature has shipped or has not?"

Section 8 — Rollout plan and reversibility

Payments features do not ship by feature flag flip. The PRD specifies the rollout shape.

  • Stage 1: internal traffic. Synthetic transactions, dogfood accounts, sandbox merchants.
  • Stage 2: reference merchants. Three to ten merchants chosen for their willingness to absorb early bugs and their representativeness of the broader portfolio.
  • Stage 3: progressive rollout. Percentage of merchants or percentage of traffic, with explicit gates between stages (KPI thresholds met, guard-rails not tripped, reconciliation clean).
  • Stage 4: GA. The point at which the legacy flow is deprecated, with a timeline for deprecation.

Reversibility is the part most teams forget. For every stage, the PRD names what "rollback" looks like and at which point rollback stops being possible without merchant-facing consequences. If the feature changes settlement timing, the rollback after settlement has occurred is not a rollback — it is a remediation, with its own runbook.

Prompt that forces clarity: "At each stage, what does it look like to stop, and what does it look like to undo what has already happened?"

Section 9 — Open questions and decisions

Every payments PRD has an open-questions section because at the moment of writing, not everything is known.

Each open question has:

  • A clear question
  • A decision owner
  • A decision deadline (date, not "ASAP")
  • The constraint the answer must satisfy

Each decided question — the ones already closed before the PRD ships to engineering — has:

  • The decision in one sentence
  • Who made it
  • Why it was made (the relevant constraint)
  • What the alternatives were

The "decisions log" is the most-read section of a payments PRD six months after launch. It is the artefact the next PM (or the audit team, or the post-incident review) uses to understand why the system behaves the way it does. Write it as if the next reader knows nothing about the room you were in when you wrote it.

Prompt that forces clarity: "If the writer of this PRD left the company tomorrow, could the next PM continue without a verbal handover?"

What is not in the template

A few sections that other PRD templates carry, that this one does not:

  • User personas. Payments products have constituencies (Section 2 of the Product Management for Payments Platforms essay), not personas. Personas leak SaaS habits into payments thinking.
  • Wireframes. Wireframes belong in design artefacts, linked from Section 7, not pasted into the PRD. The PRD is a decision document, not a design surface.
  • Engineering implementation. Implementation choices belong in engineering design docs that descend from the PRD. The PRD names the constraints, not the design.
  • Pricing model. Pricing lives in a separate commercial PRD, even when the product change has revenue implications. Mixing them produces document-shaped compromises.

How long should a payments PRD be?

A working answer: long enough that the nine sections are each substantive, and short enough that the engineering lead reads it without skipping. In practice, four to nine pages for a meaningful payments feature; one to two pages for a small change that still touches money or audit. If a PRD is over fifteen pages, it is either a strategy document pretending to be a PRD, or it should be split into two PRDs.

The senior-PM tell

The interview question that catches juniors out is not "do you write PRDs?" It is "what is in a payments PRD that is not in a SaaS PRD, and why?". The junior answer adds compliance bullets to a SaaS template. The senior answer reaches for the money-movement section, the reconciliation section, the scheme-posture section, and the open-decisions log — and explains why the SaaS template is structurally incomplete, not just under-detailed.

That answer is the difference between a PM who will ship working payment features and one who will ship incidents.

FAQ

Is this template too heavy for small changes? For very small changes (one config flip, one copy change, one telemetry-event addition), use a one-page mini-PRD that still has the nine section headings but with most of them written as one sentence. The discipline is in the section structure; the depth flexes.

Does this template work for non-card payment products? Yes. The nine sections were designed for any product that moves money — card acquiring, A2A rails, cross-border, BNPL, wallets, virtual accounts. The "scheme posture" section is generalised to "rule envelope" for products without a card scheme.

Should the PRD live in Confluence, Notion, Google Docs, or a markdown file in the repo? Whichever the engineering team reads. The platform matters less than the convention that the PRD is the single source of truth and that decisions land in the PRD rather than in chat.

Who writes each section? The PM owns the PRD. Sections 4 (regulator and scheme) and 5 (reconciliation) usually require input from compliance and finance respectively, but the PM still owns the final wording. Owning the wording is the test of whether the PM understands the constraint.

When does the PRD freeze? Never fully. The acceptance criteria and goal freeze before engineering starts; the open-questions section keeps changing; the decisions log keeps growing. A PRD is alive for the lifetime of the feature, not just the build.

How is the PRD different from a design doc? The PRD is the product decision; the design doc is the engineering choice. The PRD names the constraints; the design doc names the implementation. One PRD typically maps to one or more design docs.
