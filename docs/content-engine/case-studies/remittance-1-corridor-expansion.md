## Challenge

At a cross-border remittance provider, I was handed a mandate to launch new receive corridors at a pace the company had never attempted, in the same year the regulatory regime in our core region was being rewritten. Each corridor required a regulator's comfort, a settlement bank, payout partners, and sending-side demand, and every one of those parties was more cautious than usual precisely because the rules were in flux. The real problem was not any single approval. It was sequencing four dependent, slow-moving tracks per market, several markets at a time, without one stalled track silently killing a launch.

## Context

We focused on frontier markets, where a corridor is a dense stack rather than an integration: central bank authorization or a licensed sponsor, a settlement bank willing to serve a remittance fintech, wallet and cash payout networks for the last mile, and sending partners with real diaspora flows. Mid-transition, bank compliance committees froze new fintech onboarding to wait out the uncertainty, and regulators were slower to engage while drafting their own new frameworks. Conventional wisdom said pause expansion until the rules settled. The business could not afford to.

## Approach

We codified a corridor launch playbook with a strict critical path: regulator first, settlement bank second, payout network third, sending partners last, because each layer's confidence depends on the one before it. A bank says yes more easily when the regulator already has; payout networks sign faster when settlement is real. We parallelized across corridors but never within a critical path, and filled regulator wait times with no-regret workstreams: payout integrations, screening setup, and reporting builds that would be needed regardless of timing.

## Product Strategy

Each corridor was run as a product with launch criteria and kill criteria agreed before work began: documented diaspora demand, a payout coverage threshold, and a regulatory tractability assessment. Launches were deliberately staggered so each one's lessons were absorbed before the next began. I held one line against commercial pressure throughout: we do not launch thin. A corridor without competitive payout coverage burns the brand with senders who try once, fail their beneficiary, and never return.

## Execution

Our anchor launch nearly died when its settlement bank's compliance committee froze all new fintech accounts mid-transition, weeks from go-live, with no committed thaw date. The launch survived only because we had quietly qualified a second bank in parallel, redundancy that had been challenged as wasteful in planning and became doctrine afterward: two of everything on the critical path. The other defining moment came from a regulator who requested transaction-level reporting in a format that existed nowhere in our stack. We built it in weeks, resenting every hour, and then discovered it satisfied two other regulators almost unchanged. Sanctions and AML screening at scale was stood up before the first transaction in every market, a sequencing rule we never once regretted.

## Metrics

- 4 market launches in 2024, executed during a regulatory transition
- Footprint reaching 5 frontier-market corridors
- Each successive launch measurably faster than the previous as the playbook hardened
- Screening live from day one in every market; no compliance findings in any launch review

## Results

The playbook became the company's most reusable asset, turning corridor expansion from a heroic project into a forecastable pipeline. Regulator relationships compounded: the reporting format built under duress became evidence of good faith that opened doors in neighboring markets. Tier-1 global PSP partners began treating us as the partner of record for the region, because demonstrated launch capacity in hard markets is rare. And the kill criteria earned their keep too, sparing us one corridor whose demand evidence never materialized.

## Lessons Learned

Regulators are stakeholders to invest in, not gates to pass; the teams that engage early and over-comply during a transition are the ones remembered when licenses are granted. Redundancy on the critical path looks like waste until the week it is the launch. Demand must exist before you launch, because corridors are discovered, not created, and no payout network rescues a corridor nobody asked for. And the right pace is slower than sales wants and faster than compliance fears; if either side is fully comfortable, you have the dial wrong.

> How do you launch four remittance markets in one year during a regulatory transition? Sequence regulator, bank, payout network, then senders; qualify two of everything on the critical path; and treat every regulator request as an asset you will reuse in the next market.
