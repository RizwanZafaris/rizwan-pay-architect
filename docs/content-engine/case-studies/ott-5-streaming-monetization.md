## Challenge

Carrier billing built the subscriber base and quietly ate the business. Telco revenue share meant roughly 50% of every subscription went to the operator — acceptable tuition at launch, ruinous at scale. By the time the base reached 5M paid subscribers, the platform was scaling a margin problem: growth and viability pointed in opposite directions, and every renewal made the case for the status quo worse.

## Context

The original DCB decision had been right — in a single-digit card-penetration market it was the only rail that reached the audience. But the market had moved: mobile wallets had matured into mainstream instruments, card issuance was rising, and the platform's planned MENA expansion into UAE and KSA pointed toward card-first rails anyway. The question was no longer whether to migrate the paying base from telco billing at roughly 50% cost to wallet and card rails at roughly 1%, but how to do it without breaking the growth machine the expensive rail still powered.

## Approach

We structured it as a formal transformation programme rather than a payments project: $3M committed, five workstreams, a 25-person cross-functional team, and 8 international vendors across processing, wallet integrations, reconciliation, and migration tooling. The five workstreams: new rails and integrations; migration experience; pricing and packaging; finance operations and reconciliation; and the MENA launch, which we deliberately built on the new stack from day one rather than porting the old one.

## Product Strategy

The core strategic decision was to never force-migrate. Forced migration converts a payments change into a cancellation prompt. Instead, the wallet rails had to be genuinely better for the user: richer packaging — annual and bundle plans that 50% economics could never support — wallet-native subscription management and fast refunds as the trust layer, and price incentives that shared part of the cost difference with the customer. DCB stayed open as the acquisition rail. "Acquire on airtime, retain on wallets" became the operating doctrine. ARPU growth and cost reduction were the same motion: the packaging that lifted ARPU was only viable on 1% rails.

## Execution

Three things nearly went wrong, and one genuinely did. Early migration cohorts that we nudged too hard churned at elevated rates — we pulled back, rebuilt the prompts around value rather than urgency, and the cohorts recovered. Some vendor timelines slipped, forcing a resequencing of the rollout; the workstream structure absorbed it, which is the argument for programme discipline in the first place. The one that hurt was mid-migration reconciliation: running two rail families in parallel across operators, wallets, and acquirers buried finance in mismatches until the recon workstream — the unglamorous one nobody had fought to staff — caught up. I under-resourced it at the start and corrected late. That mistake is the one I repeat to every team I have led since.

## Metrics

- Payment cost on migrated subscribers: roughly 50% (telco revenue share) down to roughly 1% (wallet and card rails)
- ARPU: up 70%, driven by packaging only viable on low-cost rails
- Revenue scale: $10M+ ARR
- Programme: $3M, five workstreams, 25-person team, 8 international vendors
- Expansion: UAE and KSA launched on the new stack

## Results

The unit-economics turnaround changed what the business could be: from a growth story with structurally broken margins into a subscription business with defensible economics at $10M+ ARR. The migration validated the two-rail doctrine — carrier billing kept feeding the top of the funnel while wallets carried the retention economics. And MENA launched clean, with none of the legacy cost structure to unwind later.

## Lessons Learned

The rail that builds your base is rarely the rail that builds your business — start planning the second rail while you are still celebrating the first. Migration is a pricing and packaging problem wearing an integration costume; the integrations were the easier half. Never force-migrate a subscriber who is currently paying you; make the new rail worth choosing. And staff reconciliation as if the programme depends on it, because mid-migration it does — the most expensive workstream is always the one you skimped on.

> We didn't cut payment costs from 50% to 1% by negotiating harder with operators — we re-platformed the customer relationship onto rails the economics could survive.
